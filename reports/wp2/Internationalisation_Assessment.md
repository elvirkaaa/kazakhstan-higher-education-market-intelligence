# Internationalisation Assessment
## Kazakhstan Higher Education — Work Package 2

*Prepared by: AZM Education — Internationalisation & Analytics Research*
*Status: Draft, based on sources processed to date (see Limitations, Section 10, for what remains open)*

This report structures the findings already established in `docs/wp2/WP2_Analytical_Notes.md` (source-by-source analysis) and `analysis/wp2/WP2_Insights.md` (insight mapping) into a single narrative. It does not introduce new analysis beyond what those two documents already support.

---

## 1. Introduction

Work Package 1 (Higher Education Landscape) established what Kazakhstan's higher education system offers — its institutions, programmes, pricing, and English-medium provision. Work Package 2 asks a different question: **how connected is that system internationally?** — who studies in Kazakhstan and from where, where Kazakhstani students and scholars go, which countries are Kazakhstan's strongest partners, and how that connectivity has evolved.

This assessment draws on six sources: a translated report on CIS academic mobility (2022), UN DESA migrant stock data, a Bologna Process monitoring report (2023), Kazakhstan's National Report on the State and Development of Higher Education (2024, Section 6 — Internationalisation), a peer-reviewed article on qualification recognition, and an existing dashboard built on UNESCO UIS data (not yet independently re-verified). Full source detail is in `docs/wp2/05_Data_Sources.md`.

## 2. Purpose

To assess the current state of Kazakhstan's higher education internationalisation — inbound and outbound student/faculty mobility, scholarship and grant programmes, institutional partnerships, and qualification recognition — as a foundation for identifying opportunities for further international engagement.

## 3. Data & Methodology

Full methodology is documented in `docs/wp2/03_Methodology.md`. In summary: every source was transcribed into single-topic, verifiable spreadsheets before any narrative claim was drawn from it; internal arithmetic was checked wherever a table's rows should sum to a stated total; and where two sources covered the same metric, they were cross-checked against each other. Confirmed inconsistencies are documented in the underlying files and are not silently corrected. One cross-check (CIS report vs. National Report, 2022 CIS-country mobility) is described in Section 9.4 below as a worked example of this process.

## 4. Inbound Academic Mobility

In 2024, 1,207 foreign students participated in academic mobility programmes at Kazakhstani universities — a slight decline (-2%) from 1,226 in 2023, continuing a downward trend from 1,971 in 2021. The large majority (82%) studied offline. Bachelor's-level students dominate (1,084 of 1,207), with credit academic mobility the most common mobility type (1,153 of 1,207).

Kyrgyzstan was the single largest source country in 2024 (411 students, 34% of inbound mobility), overtaking Russia (219), which had led in earlier years — a reversal worth monitoring rather than treating as an established trend (see Insight #8, `WP2_Insights.md`). CIS countries as a group remain the dominant source region (788 of 1,207 in 2024), followed by South/Southeast Asia (181, led by China) and Europe (221, with no single dominant country).

A separate and much larger population — foreign students enrolled as full-time degree students, rather than short-term mobility participants — numbered over 26,000 as of the mid-2020s, with India the clear leader (12,020 in 2024) and concentrated overwhelmingly (99.8%) in medical specialties at a specific set of medical universities (see Section 6).

## 5. Outbound Academic Mobility

Outbound mobility (Kazakhstani students studying abroad, outside the Bolashak scholarship programme — see Section 8) reached 4,416 students in 2024, roughly flat versus 4,426 in 2023 but up substantially from 3,246 in 2021. Europe is the leading destination region (2,360 in 2024, 54% of the total), driven by Turkey (792, regaining the top single-country spot) and Poland (691). CIS destinations fell 26% year-on-year (1,902 to 1,402), while South/Southeast Asian destinations grew 56% (326 to 598, led by South Korea and China).

This regional shift — away from CIS, toward Europe and, to a lesser extent, Asia — is currently a single year of directional change and should be treated as an emerging signal rather than an established trend (Insight #7).

## 6. Foreign Students: Level, Region, and Field Concentration

Beyond mobility-specific figures, Kazakhstan hosts a large full-degree foreign student population, reported at roughly 26,000–28,500 depending on year and measurement basis (see the data-quality note on this figure in `Foreign_Students_by_Level_2020_2024.xlsx`, which flags a suspicious repeated total across 2020–2023 that should be verified against the original source before being relied upon).

India's dominance is almost entirely a medical-education phenomenon: of 12,020 Indian students in 2024, 11,997 (99.8%) study at one of twelve identified universities, most bearing "Medical University" in their name (e.g. S.D. Asfendiyarov Kazakh National Medical University, Karaganda Medical University). This is one of the most concentrated, well-evidenced patterns in the entire WP2 dataset (Insight #2).

## 7. Faculty and Institutional Exchange

Kazakhstani faculty participating in academic exchange rose from 614 (2023) to 769 (2024), with state universities accounting for the largest and fastest-growing share (30.9% to 44.6%). The destination-country list for faculty exchange spans 47 countries in 2024 (up from 33 in 2023), though the underlying country-level percentage figures contain several unresolved anomalies (see `Faculty_Exchange_by_Country_2023_2024.xlsx`) that should be verified before being cited externally.

On the CIS-specific institutional layer, Russia is disproportionately dominant: it accounts for 65% of all Kazakhstan–CIS bilateral cooperation agreements (2,053 of 3,172) and 71% of hired (extrabudgetary-funded) foreign specialists from CIS countries — both shares higher than Russia's share of CIS-origin student mobility itself (Insight #4).

## 8. Institutional Partnerships and Branch Campuses

Kazakhstan has established 40 strategic partnerships with foreign universities, including 33 active branch campuses across 11 partner countries (Russia, USA, UK, China, South Korea, France, Germany, Italy, Poland, Turkey, Uzbekistan), offering 162 educational programmes. Of these, 26 are currently functioning, with 8 more opening in 2025 and 6 in 2026.

Notable recent additions include Cardiff University Kazakhstan (2025) - Cardiff's first overseas campus and the only Russell Group university presence in Central Asia - and Coventry University Kazakhstan (2024), the first British university in Astana. Investment partners include Kazakhstani industrial firms (e.g. Kazphosphate LLP supporting the RKhTU branch) and foreign government-linked funders (China funding equipment and an engineering centre at D. Serikbayev East Kazakhstan Technical University; South Korea funding an AI institute at Korkyt Ata University via SeoulTech).

This institutional partnership layer draws on a notably different country set than student mobility itself: South Korea, Italy, Poland, and Hungary all appear as meaningful branch-campus partners but do not feature prominently in the mobility-specific data (Sections 4-5). This suggests Kazakhstan's internationalisation strategy operates across at least three distinct channels - organic student mobility (CIS/Asia-dominated), elite outbound scholarships (Anglophone-West-dominated, Section 9 below), and institutional partnerships (a broader, more geographically diverse set) - rather than a single coherent country strategy (Insight #13).

Kazakhstan's government has set a target of 100,000 international students by 2029, roughly three times the current reported figure of 35,057 - a target that sits in some tension with the recent decline in mobility-specific inbound student numbers documented in Section 4 (Insight #14).

## 9. Scholarships and Grant Programmes

**Bolashak.** As of end-2024, the Bolashak programme has produced 12,727 cumulative graduates, overwhelmingly at Master's level (57.5%) and destined for the UK/Ireland (45.5%) or USA/Canada (28%) — together 73.5% of all Bolashak graduates. Of these, 10,228 have fulfilled their contractual work-placement obligations. This Anglophone-West orientation stands in direct contrast to the CIS/Asia orientation of general inbound mobility (Section 4), forming one of the clearest structural asymmetries identified in this work package (Insight #3).

**Intergovernmental and interdepartmental grants.** Two distinct grant channels fund Kazakhstani citizens' study abroad under bilateral/multilateral agreements. Both channels are led by Hungary and China as top destinations — neither channel lists a CIS country as its top destination, reinforcing the pattern that state-sponsored outbound study targets a different partner-country set than organic inbound mobility (Insight #6).

**Research Internships.** In 2024, 250 scholarships were awarded (of 817 applications, per the formal application-funnel table), with Engineering & Technology (104) and Social Sciences (44) the leading fields, and the USA (244 graduates) the leading host country.

## 10. European Union Partnerships (Erasmus+)

Kazakhstani university participation in Erasmus+ mechanisms grew substantially in 2024: PPVO applications rose 36% (47 to 64 submitted) and Jean Monnet applications rose 52% (35 to 53 submitted). Poland, Turkey, Spain, and Romania are the leading EU/associated partner countries for International Credit Mobility projects. KIMEP University won Kazakhstan and Central Asia's first Erasmus Mundus Design Measure project in 2024 (GreenMAS, a joint Master's programme in green economy management).

Despite this growth, EU-oriented activity remains small in absolute terms relative to CIS-oriented mobility — a handful of selected projects and a few hundred participants, against thousands of CIS-origin mobility students in the same year (Insight #10).

## 11. Recognition of Foreign Qualifications

Recognition/nostrification applications have grown from 10,524 (2017) to a peak of 23,372 (2022), settling at 18,557 in 2025. The same five countries — Russia, Uzbekistan, India, China, and Kyrgyzstan — have consistently accounted for the largest shares of applications throughout this period, mirroring the country concentration seen in academic mobility and migrant stock data (Insight #9). This convergence across three independently sourced datasets (recognition, mobility, migration) is one of the more robust patterns identified in WP2.

## 12. Migration Context

Kazakhstan's general migrant stock (not education-specific) is led by the same top countries as student mobility — Uzbekistan (744,161 in 2024) and Russia (552,258) — but at a scale two to three orders of magnitude larger than education-specific flows. This confirms that student mobility, while directionally aligned with broader migration patterns, is a small and distinct slice of Kazakhstan's overall migration picture, not a proxy for it (Insight #5).

## 13. Cross-Source Verification: A Worked Example

As a demonstration of this work package's verification standard, the 2022 CIS-country mobility figures were checked between the CIS report and the National Report. Incoming mobility reconciled exactly across every country and the total (1,080 = 1,080) — the strongest independent confirmation found anywhere in WP2. Outgoing mobility reconciled for four of seven countries; Kyrgyzstan did not (89 in the CIS report vs. 127 in the National Report). Testing which combination of values reconciles each source's own stated total (1,602) showed that using the National Report's Kyrgyzstan figure (127) alongside the CIS report's Armenia and Tajikistan figures (1 and 9, both blank in the National Report) reconciles exactly — strong evidence that the CIS report's "89" is a transcription error. This finding is documented in full, with the supporting arithmetic, in the relevant processed files.

## 14. Strategic Observations

Drawing together Sections 4–11, three structural patterns stand out:

1. **A bifurcated internationalisation strategy.** Kazakhstan's state-sponsored elite outbound programmes (Bolashak, intergovernmental/interdepartmental grants) systematically target Western/non-CIS partners (UK, USA, Hungary, China), while organic inbound mobility and full-degree foreign enrolment are systematically CIS/Asia-dominated. These appear to be two largely separate internationalisation channels rather than one coherent strategy (Insights #3, #6).
2. **Deep but narrow country concentration.** Across mobility, recognition, and migration data alike, the same handful of countries — Russia, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, India, China — recur as the dominant partners, with Russia's institutional-cooperation footprint (agreements, hired staff) exceeding even its already-large student-mobility footprint (Insights #1, #4, #9).
3. **A small but fast-growing EU channel.** Erasmus+ engagement is growing at double-digit percentage rates annually but remains a minor share of total international activity — a potential area for deliberate expansion rather than one that will scale on its own momentum at the current pace (Insight #10).
4. **Institutional partnerships reach further than student flows.** Branch-campus and strategic-partnership activity spans a broader country set (South Korea, Italy, Poland, Hungary) than either organic inbound mobility or elite outbound scholarships, suggesting Kazakhstan is building institutional relationships ahead of - rather than in direct response to - actual student flow patterns (Insight #13).

## 15. Limitations

- **UNESCO UIS has not yet been independently verified.** All UIS-related content in this report derives from an existing dashboard, not a directly queried source.
- **The National Report 2024 (the richest single source) has been processed only for the sections supplied so far** via user-provided screenshots; further sections may exist and could alter or add to the findings above.
- **Multiple confirmed internal data-quality issues** affect specific figures cited in this report (see the per-file notes in `data/processed/wp2/`), including: a likely chart-labelling artifact in the foreign-students-by-level figure; unresolved percentage-base ambiguities in the Bolashak status breakdown; several percentage/count mismatches in the faculty exchange by-country table; and an unresolved discrepancy between two of the National Report's own outgoing-mobility totals (now provisionally resolved in favour of 4,426 for 2023 — see `docs/wp2/06_Data_Processing_Log.md`).
- **This report is entirely supply-side / activity-side.** It describes flows and programme participation as reported by Kazakhstani institutions and government sources; it does not independently verify these figures against destination-country records (e.g. verifying Kazakhstani student numbers in Turkey against Turkish government data), nor does it capture unmet demand.
- **A third official Kazakhstani source (MSHE branch-campus press material) introduces yet another set of foreign-student figures that conflicts with the National Report** - India ranges from 9,969 to 12,020 across two sources, and the MSHE source's own claim that "Asia overtook CIS in 2024" contradicts its own printed numbers (CIS 17,816 vs. Asia 17,788). This is now a confirmed, recurring pattern (Insight #15) rather than an isolated error, and materially limits confidence in any single foreign-student headline figure until a primary statistical release can adjudicate between them.
- **Single-year directional claims** (Kyrgyzstan overtaking Russia; the CIS-to-Europe outbound shift) are based on one year of change and are flagged as such throughout; they should not be treated as established trends.

## 16. Conclusion and Next Steps

Kazakhstan's higher education system shows meaningful but structurally uneven internationalisation: strong, longstanding CIS integration at the organic/institutional level, a distinct and Western-oriented elite scholarship channel, a fast-growing but still small EU partnership channel, and a foreign-student population whose scale is dominated by a single country-field combination (Indian medical students). Recognition of foreign qualifications tracks the same country concentration seen elsewhere, reinforcing confidence in the overall pattern despite the data-quality caveats above.

This assessment feeds directly into the next stages of the Kazakhstan Higher Education Market Intelligence programme: WP3 (Demographic Outlook) can test whether Kazakhstan's own demographic trends help explain the inbound/outbound asymmetry described here, and WP4 (Labour Market Alignment) can test whether the country-partner pattern identified in Section 13 aligns with labour-market outcomes for internationally mobile graduates.
