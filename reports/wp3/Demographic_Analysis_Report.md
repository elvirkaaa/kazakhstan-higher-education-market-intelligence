# Demographic Analysis Report
## Kazakhstan Higher Education — Work Package 3

*Prepared by: AZM Education — Internationalisation & Analytics Research*
*Status: Draft, based on sources processed to date (see Limitations, Section 10)*

This report structures the findings already established in `analysis/WP3_Analytical_Notes.md` (source-by-source analysis) and `analysis/WP3_Insights.md` (insight mapping, including cross-work-package Insights #12-16) into a single narrative. It does not introduce new analysis beyond what those two documents already support.

## 1. Executive Summary

Work Package 1 (Higher Education Landscape) established what Kazakhstan's higher education system offers. Work Package 2 (Internationalisation) established how connected that system is internationally. Work Package 3 asks a different question: **what demographic forces will shape demand for that system going forward** — and, critically, **where**.

Three headline findings stand out. First, Kazakhstan's population has grown consistently since the mid-1990s and is projected to keep growing to at least 2050, but the university-applicant-age cohort (15-24) declined for three decades before a projected reversal beginning around 2024-2026. Second, the total dependency ratio — after falling for most of the 1990s-2010s — is now rising again, suggesting Kazakhstan may be approaching the end of a favourable demographic window. Third, and most consequential for strategic planning: **higher-education supply is not distributed according to where Kazakhstan's youth population actually is**. Turkistan region has the country's youngest population (39.5% aged 0-15, the national high) yet holds only 1.0% of national higher-education programme supply (WP1 finding), while Almaty — with a below-average youth share (27.4%) — holds 71.6% of supply.

## 2. Introduction

WP3 covers general population and migration context only — international student mobility, Bolashak, Erasmus+, and foreign university partnerships remain part of WP2 and are not duplicated here. Full scope and research questions are in `reports/wp3/Research Design.md`.

## 3. Data Sources and Methodology

Three primary sources were used, each covering a distinct gap: the Bureau of National Statistics (BNS) for Kazakhstan's own official demographic and migration statistics; the UN World Population Prospects (UN WPP), via the Population Division Data Portal, for projections, dependency ratio, and youth-cohort age bands; and World Bank Open Data for long, internationally standardised time series on population, growth, urbanisation, and fertility. Full source-by-source detail is in `docs/Data_Sources.md` (WP3 section) and `analysis/WP3_Analytical_Notes.md`.

One methodological point deserves emphasis: a sustained, direct attempt was made to obtain a combined regional × age-band (ideally 17-24) population time series via TALDAU, Kazakhstan's official interactive statistics portal. This was **confirmed, through direct testing, not to be exportable** — TALDAU's export function for the relevant indicator allows only one classification dimension (region OR age group) per export, not both simultaneously. As a result, this report answers youth-cohort questions (Section 8) at the **national level**, and regional questions (Section 9) using population, migration, and a coarser 3-band age structure, presented side by side rather than forced into a single unsupported metric. Full detail is in `reports/wp3/Research Design.md`.

## 4. Historical Demographic Trends

Kazakhstan's population fell through most of the 1990s following independence — a period of significant emigration — before beginning a sustained recovery. By 2025, the population had reached approximately 20.8 million, comfortably above the pre-independence level. This recovery has been driven by a combination of positive natural increase (Section 5) and, in more recent years, a less negative external migration balance (Section 6).

## 5. Population Growth and Natural Increase

Kazakhstan's annual population growth rate turned negative during the 1990s emigration wave before recovering to a consistently positive rate (generally in the 1-1.5% range) from the early-to-mid 2000s onward. Births have consistently exceeded deaths for most of the observed period (1991-2025), producing positive natural increase in most years — though both the birth rate and the death rate have fluctuated considerably, including a visible disruption around 2020-2021 consistent with the global pandemic period. Total fertility has declined steadily from 4.5 births per woman (1960) to under 3.0 (2024), the classic signature of a demographic transition, and this decline is mirrored in the long fall of the total dependency ratio through the 1990s-2010s (Section 7).

## 6. Migration Context

Kazakhstan's external migration balance was substantially negative in the early 2000s (net outflow) before moderating considerably and, in some more recent years, turning positive. This general migration context should not be conflated with WP2's international-student mobility findings — the two measure different populations and, as WP2 documented, move at very different scales (general migrant stock runs 200-500x larger than education-specific mobility for the same country pairs).

Internally, interregional migration shows a clear and consistent pattern: Astana, Almaty, and Shymkent — the three republican-significance cities — draw the largest net internal in-migration (+87,459 / +33,126 / +15,130 respectively, most recent available year), while Turkistan region and Zhambyl show the largest net internal out-migration (-42,649 / -21,179). This concentration toward major cities is consistent with, and likely reinforces, the higher-education supply concentration documented in WP1 (Insight #14).

## 7. Population Projections

UN World Population Prospects projects Kazakhstan's population to continue growing through at least 2050, reaching approximately 26.5 million under the medium-fertility variant — a further ~28% increase from the 2025 level. The total dependency ratio, after its long historical decline, is projected to continue rising through the 2020s, meaning each working-age person will, on average, support more dependents than in the low-point years of the 2000s-2010s.

Most relevant to this project's core question: the 15-24 cohort — the population from which Kazakhstan's universities draw their applicant pool — declined from 2.93 million (1990) to a low of approximately 2.82 million (2024), before UN WPP's projection shows an increase to roughly 3.08 million by 2026 (Insight #4). This reversal is corroborated, for the single overlapping year of 2025, by an independent BNS/TALDAU extraction that closely matches UN WPP's own figures (within 1.3% for the 15-19 band and 0.2% for the 20-24 band — Insight #6), which increases confidence in both series. Whether this reversal continues through the longer 2030-2050 horizon has not yet been checked against the available projection data and remains an open question for future work.

It is also worth noting that Kazakhstan's own official statistical definition of "youth" (14-28 years, per national legislation) is considerably broader than the international 15-24 standard used elsewhere in this project (Insight #5) — both definitions have a legitimate place in a Kazakhstan-focused analysis and are used accordingly throughout this report.

## 8. Regional Demographic Differences

Population is heavily concentrated in a small number of regions: Almaty city, Turkistan region, and Almaty region are among the largest by raw population, alongside Astana city and Zhambyl region. But raw population size tells only part of the story — age structure varies considerably by region. Using the coarser 3-band age classification available at regional level (child / working-age / pension-age, beginning of 2024), Turkistan region has the highest child (0-15) population share of any region nationally (39.5%, against a 31.2% national average), followed by Mangystau (38.7%), Shymkent city (37.4%), Kyzylorda (36.4%), and Atyrau (35.8%). At the other end, North Kazakhstan (21.5%), Kostanay (22.6%), and East Kazakhstan (22.8%) show the lowest child population shares — an older regional population profile.

This regional age variation is the foundation for the report's most significant finding, developed fully in Section 9.

## 9. Implications for Higher Education

This section connects WP3's demographic findings directly to WP1's higher-education supply data (8,530 raw OPVO records, ~2,417 distinct programmes, 46 universities) — see `analysis/WP3_Insights.md`, Insights #12-16, for the full evidentiary detail behind each point below.

**Finding 1 — Supply is geographically inverted relative to youth concentration.** Turkistan region combines the country's highest child population share (39.5%) with one of the thinnest higher-education footprints in WP1's dataset (1.0% of national programme supply). This is not a marginal gap: a region containing close to 40% child-age population commands roughly one-hundredth of the country's higher-education programme supply.

**Finding 2 — The second-largest supply region is not a young-population region.** Ust-Kamenogorsk / East Kazakhstan holds 10.0% of national programme supply — second only to Almaty — despite having the second-lowest child population share of any region (22.8%). Historical/institutional legacy, not current demographic demand, appears to be the better explanation for this region's supply position.

**Finding 3 — Almaty's dominance is not a demography story.** Almaty city's youth (0-15) share (27.4%) sits below the national average, yet Almaty holds 71.6% of national programme supply. Combined with Almaty's position as a top internal-migration destination (Section 6), this suggests Almaty's dominance reflects agglomeration and migration-pull dynamics more than local demographic demand — people, including prospective students, appear to move toward existing supply rather than supply following the population.

**Finding 4 — A shrinking national applicant pool may have reinforced concentration rather than caused it (interpretive, lower confidence).** Kazakhstan's 15-24 cohort declined for three decades (Section 7). A shrinking national pool of applicants makes small, dispersed regional expansions harder to sustain economically than consolidating supply in one large hub — a plausible contributing mechanism to WP1's finding that regional concentration (HHI 6,008) far exceeds institutional concentration (HHI 1,000), though this is an interpretive link between two datasets, not a directly tested causal claim.

**Finding 5 — If the projected youth-cohort reversal continues, it will arrive disproportionately in under-supplied regions.** The regions with the youngest current populations (Turkistan, Mangystau, Kyzylorda, Atyrau, Shymkent city — all above 35% child population share) are simultaneously among the thinnest in higher-education supply. A rebounding national 15-24 cohort (Section 7) would, on current regional demographic patterns, materialise disproportionately in exactly the regions least equipped to serve it locally.

## 10. Limitations

- **No regional × age-band cross-tabulation exists in an exportable form** from Kazakhstan's official statistics portal (TALDAU) for the relevant indicator — confirmed through direct testing, not assumed. Regional youth-specific findings in this report rely on a coarser 3-band age classification (child/working-age/pension-age), not a precise 17-24 or 15-24 regional breakdown.
- **Only a single-year (2025) regional population snapshot has been obtained** — a genuine multi-year regional population time series was not available, limiting the ability to assess which regions are growing or shrinking in population terms specifically (as opposed to age structure or migration).
- **The three regions created in the 2022 territorial reform** (Abai, Zhetisu, Ulytau) have only 2-4 years of independent data in every BNS time series used in this report — too little to assess long-run trends for these regions specifically.
- **The interregional migration data's reference year is not explicitly stated** in the source file — inferred as most likely 2024 or 2025 from file metadata, not confirmed.
- **The 2030-2050 population projection was transcribed from a Worldometer screenshot** (itself an elaboration of UN WPP 2024 Revision data), not downloaded directly from the UN Data Portal — a direct download would be more authoritative if obtained.
- **The exact historical-estimate-vs-projection cutoff in the UN WPP Data Portal exports is not explicitly labelled** — years 2024-2026 are treated as likely short-term projections based on general WPP methodology, not a stated fact in the export itself.
- **The cross-work-package findings in Section 9 combine two independently collected datasets (WP1 and WP3)** and, while the underlying percentages are each precise and directly sourced, the interpretive links between them (particularly Finding 4) should be read as plausible hypotheses rather than proven causal relationships.
- **A planned cross-check of BNS vs. World Bank national population totals has not yet been completed.**

## 11. Conclusions

Kazakhstan's demographic trajectory — sustained population growth, a historically low but now rising dependency ratio, and a university-applicant-age cohort that may be beginning to grow again after three decades of decline — sets a broadly favourable backdrop for the higher-education sector's continued development. But the regional dimension complicates a simple growth narrative: the country's youngest, fastest-growing-by-composition regions (Turkistan, Mangystau, Kyzylorda, Atyrau, Shymkent city) are systematically under-served by current higher-education supply, while supply remains concentrated in regions (Almaty, East Kazakhstan) whose demographic profiles do not obviously justify that concentration on demand grounds alone.

This is the clearest, most actionable finding to emerge from WP3: **future higher-education demand growth in Kazakhstan is more likely to be regional than national** — concentrated in exactly the parts of the country current supply has least prepared to serve. This finding should carry directly into WP4 (Labour Market Alignment), which can test whether these same under-supplied, high-youth-share regions also show labour-market signals (e.g. skills shortages, employer demand) consistent with a genuine, economically-grounded case for expanded regional higher-education investment — and into the final integrated Kazakhstan Higher Education Market Intelligence report, where the WP1×WP3 regional mismatch identified here deserves a dedicated strategic section.
