# Executive Summary

This report assesses the structure, pricing, and internationalisation profile of Kazakhstan's higher education market, drawing on a nationally sourced registry of academic programmes maintained under the Official/Electronic Programme classification system (OPVO/EPVO). It is written for international universities, investors, and partners who are unfamiliar with Kazakhstan and are evaluating whether, where, and how to engage with its higher education sector.

The underlying dataset comprises 8,530 programme-level records from 46 universities, which resolve to approximately 2,417 distinct degree programmes once duplicate registry entries are removed. Programmes span the full academic pathway — Bachelor's, Master's, Doctoral, and medical Residency — across eleven broad subject areas, priced in Kazakhstani tenge (KZT) for both domestic and international students.

### What we found

- The market is highly city-concentrated: Almaty alone accounts for roughly seven in ten distinct programmes and nearly half of all universities in the dataset, while most other regions offer only one or two institutions each.

- English-medium instruction covers just over a third of distinct programmes nationally, but this figure conceals very wide variation — from over three-quarters of Information & Communication Technology programmes to roughly one in ten Agriculture and Veterinary programmes.

- Tuition fees rise consistently with degree level (Bachelor's to Doctoral) and carry a measurable, though inconsistent, premium for English-medium instruction — the premium reverses direction in roughly a quarter of comparable subject/level combinations.

- Competitive intensity varies sharply by field: Business, Economics & Law and Engineering & Technology are the most contested subject areas by provider count, while Architecture & Urban Planning, Healthcare & Medicine, and Agriculture & Veterinary Sciences have the fewest providers nationally.

- The graduate-level (Master's and Doctoral) market outside Almaty is very thin, pointing to a specific, evidence-backed opportunity for international postgraduate provision in secondary cities.

### Why it matters

For an international institution, these patterns translate into concrete, differentiated entry questions: which city, which subject area, which degree level, and which pricing tier offer the clearest path to a defensible position rather than direct competition with an entrenched incumbent. The analysis in this report is built to support exactly that decision, while being explicit about the limits of the underlying data.

### How to read this report

Sections 4–10 build the evidentiary base (market structure, institutions, programmes, English-medium provision, pricing, regions, and segments). Section 11 distils this into named strategic insights; Sections 12–13 translate those insights into opportunities, risks, and audience-specific recommendations. Section 14 states the limitations of the dataset plainly, and the Appendices provide full technical detail for readers who wish to verify any individual figure.

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background

Kazakhstan is Central Asia's largest economy and the region's largest higher education market by enrolment, with a university system that combines Soviet-era research institutes, newly established private universities, and a small but growing cohort of English-medium and internationally partnered institutions. Over the past decade, the government has pursued an active internationalisation agenda — expanding English-medium instruction, digitising programme registries for transparency, and encouraging partnerships with foreign universities — while domestic demand for higher education continues to grow alongside the country's young population.

For an international university considering Kazakhstan, the market presents an unusual mix of opportunity and opacity. Programme-level data exists (via the national OPVO/EPVO classification and registry), but it is fragmented, inconsistently maintained, and rarely analysed in aggregate. This creates a genuine information gap: institutions with strong sector fundamentals may be systematically underestimated simply because no consolidated market view has existed until now.

## 1.2 Purpose

This report consolidates that fragmented registry data into a single, analysable market view, and translates it into decision-relevant findings for an international audience with no prior exposure to Kazakhstan's higher education system. The purpose is not merely descriptive: every section is built to answer a market-entry question — where is the market open, where is it saturated, and what would a credible, differentiated entry look like.

## 1.3 Scope

In scope:

- All programme-level records captured in the OPVO/EPVO-based registry compiled for this study — 8,530 raw records covering 46 universities and approximately 2,417 distinct programmes.

- Degree levels: Bachelor's, Master's, Doctoral (PhD), and medical Residency.

- Tuition fees for domestic (RK-citizen) and, where available, international students, denominated in KZT.

- English-medium instruction status, subject area classification, and inferred regional location for each university.

Out of scope:

- Actual student enrolment, application, or acceptance-rate data (the registry captures programme offerings and list prices, not demand).

- Accreditation status, quality rankings, or graduate employment outcomes.

- Non-degree, vocational, or short-course provision.

- Real-time currency conversion — all fee comparisons are presented in KZT unless otherwise noted (see Appendix D).

# 2. Research Background

This section explains why a systematic market view of Kazakhstan's higher education sector is now both necessary and newly feasible — distinct from the data itself, which is addressed in Section 3.

## 2.1 Internationalisation as a national priority

Kazakhstan's higher education policy has, for over a decade, prioritised measurable internationalisation: growth in English-medium provision, expansion of joint and dual-degree arrangements, and digitisation of programme registries to improve transparency for both domestic applicants and foreign partners. This report's dataset is itself a product of that transparency push, and its structure — programme-level pricing, English-medium flags, and formal classification codes — reflects the categories the Kazakhstani system itself considers decision-relevant.

## 2.2 A growing, price-differentiated domestic market

Kazakhstan's university-age population and overall demand for higher education have continued to grow, supported by state scholarship programmes (grants) alongside a large fee-paying segment. This has produced a highly price-differentiated market — from heavily subsidised regional pedagogical institutes to premium private universities charging several times the national median — which is a central theme of Sections 8 and 10 of this report.

## 2.3 The role of English as a market signal

English-medium instruction functions in this market less as a universal standard and more as a segmentation variable: it correlates with subject area, institution type, and price tier in ways that are directly relevant to how an international university would position itself. Section 7 treats English-medium provision as its own analytical chapter for this reason, rather than folding it into the general programme landscape.

## 2.4 Why a consolidated view has not existed until now

The underlying registry data is published at a granular, per-programme level and is not natively aggregated into a market-level view; nor does it carry a native region or institution-type field. Constructing the market view in this report therefore required the data consolidation, cleaning, and inference work documented in Section 3 and Appendix A.

# 3. Data & Methodology

## 3.1 Sources

- Primary source: the OPVO/EPVO programme classification registry, which lists formally registered degree programmes, their classification codes, and domestic/international tuition fees.

- A standalone HTML registry tool was built to structure and query the underlying registry data at scale, replicating the platform's programme-level structure across all 46 universities studied.

- Institution locations (regions) were inferred from each university's publicly known home city, since the source registry does not carry a native region field (see 3.3 and Appendix A for the full mapping and its limitations).

## 3.2 Dataset

The analysis dataset comprises 8,530 programme-level records across 14 fields: a registry index, an OPVO classification code, programme name, registration status, university name, degree level, an update-status flag, registration date, domestic (RK-citizen) tuition fee, foreign-fee currency, foreign-student tuition fee, English-medium flag, pricing academic year, and subject area classification.

## 3.3 Processing

Three processing stages were applied before analysis:

- Cleaning: correction of column misalignment in the source export; identification and normalisation of a Cyrillic/Latin character-encoding inconsistency affecting roughly 3% of programme codes; validation of date formats.

- Deduplication: the raw registry contains substantial row-level duplication — 87% of raw rows sit inside groups sharing an identical university, classification code, and degree level, most plausibly arising from repeated registry export/ingestion rather than genuine repeated registration events. All market-size and frequency statistics in this report use the deduplicated programme count unless explicitly labelled “raw records”.

- Standardisation and inference: institution names were reviewed for malformed strings; each university was mapped to an inferred home region using publicly available information, since no native region field exists in the source data. This mapping is an analytical assumption, not a verified registry field, and is disclosed in full in Appendix A.

## 3.4 Limitations of the methodology

These limitations recur throughout the report and are consolidated fully in Section 14; the most consequential are flagged here because they condition how every subsequent statistic should be read:

- Region is an inferred, not native, field.

- OPVO classification codes are not guaranteed unique across institutions — the same code has been observed attached to different programmes at different universities — so codes should not be used as a sole cross-reference key.

- A subset of institutions (13 of 43 priced universities) show zero price variance across every listed programme, consistent with either a genuine flat institutional fee or an unpopulated per-programme pricing field; this report flags these institutions wherever they materially affect a finding.

- International (foreign-student) fee data is the least complete field in the dataset (see Section 8.4).

# 5. Institutional Landscape

This section profiles the 46 universities in the dataset as a competitive set: their relative scale, ownership signals, specialisation, and pricing behaviour.

## 5.1 Scale distribution

Institutional scale is highly uneven. The largest institution by programme count — a large state technical research university — alone contributes roughly a quarter of all distinct programmes in the dataset, while a long tail of smaller private and regional institutions contribute a handful of programmes each. The concentration ratio (top four institutions' combined share) sits close to 50% of all raw registry rows, though this compresses somewhat once deduplication is applied, since the largest institutions also carry the heaviest raw-row duplication.

## 5.2 Ownership and type signals

The dataset does not carry a native ownership-type field, but institution names carry a partial signal: state institutions typically append a legal form such as NPJSC (non-profit joint-stock company) or RSE (state enterprise), while private institutions are typically registered as LLP or JSC, or simply carry a brand name (e.g., ALMAU, Turan, UIB, KIMEP). This naming convention is used descriptively in this report but has not been independently verified against a corporate registry, and should be treated as indicative rather than authoritative.

## 5.3 Specialisation versus breadth

Institutions in this market follow one of two structurally distinct models, measurable directly from the subject-area mix of their programme catalogue:

- Generalist / scale institutions — typified by the largest research university in the dataset, which spreads its programme catalogue across many subject areas (no single area exceeds half of its catalogue) while still accounting for the largest raw share of the market.

- Specialist / niche institutions — several smaller institutions concentrate 95–100% of their catalogue in a single subject area (for example, dedicated arts academies and a dedicated medical academy), giving them an uncontested position within a narrow field at the cost of overall scale.

No institution in the dataset occupies a stable middle ground of moderate scale with moderate specialisation — the market bifurcates cleanly between these two models, a pattern developed further in Section 10.

## 5.4 Pricing behaviour by institution

Institutional pricing behaviour also splits into two distinct patterns. The majority of priced institutions (30 of 43 with sufficient data) show genuine per-programme price variation, with within-institution price dispersion (coefficient of variation) ranging from near-uniform to highly differentiated. A minority — 13 institutions — show exactly zero price variance: every listed programme at that institution carries an identical tuition figure. As noted in Section 3.4, this may reflect either a genuine flat institutional fee policy or an incomplete per-programme pricing field, and should be treated with appropriate caution when used as a competitor benchmark.

# 6. Academic Programme Landscape

This chapter examines the composition of the programme catalogue itself — what is taught, at what level, and how concentrated specific programme titles are across the market.

## 6.1 Most common programme titles

A relatively small number of programme titles recur very frequently across institutions, indicating the most commoditised, least differentiated segments of the market. The most-repeated titles in the raw registry are Marketing, Biotechnology, Pedagogy and Psychology, Software Engineering, and Management — each appearing under the same or a near-identical title at dozens of institutions.

## 6.2 Degree-level completeness by institution

Very few institutions offer the complete Bachelor’s-through-Doctoral pathway. Residency-level (medical) programmes, in particular, are concentrated in only two regions nationally (see Section 9), meaning that for most subject areas and most cities, an applicant cannot progress through a full academic pathway without relocating.

## 6.3 A structural data caveat: classification-code reuse

A material finding for any reader intending to use this registry’s classification codes for further cross-referencing: OPVO codes are not guaranteed unique across institutions. Verified examples include a single code shared between an MBA programme at one university and an unrelated Marketing programme at another, and a single code shared between a Business Law programme and an unrelated International Law programme at two different institutions. Programme identity should therefore always be confirmed by institution name and programme title jointly, never by classification code alone.

## 6.4 Cross-disciplinary and applied programmes

A visible minority of programmes combine a technical or scientific base with business, legal, or creative-industry framing — for example, mining-sector MBAs, engineering-adjacent management degrees, and applied media/technology combinations. These programmes are too few in number to quantify as a distinct market segment with confidence, but their presence suggests institutions are experimenting with differentiated positioning within otherwise crowded core disciplines (see Section 6.1).

# 7. English-Medium Education

English-medium instruction is treated as its own chapter because it is the single most direct point of relevance for an international institution evaluating this market, and because it behaves as a segmentation variable rather than a uniform standard.

## 7.1 National picture

35.4% of distinct programmes are delivered in English (raw-record share is somewhat higher, at 47.3%, reflecting heavier raw-row duplication among English-medium programmes at large technical institutions — see Section 3.3). Both figures point to the same conclusion: English-medium provision is a minority, not a majority, of the market.

## 7.2 Variation by subject area

| **Subject area**                         | **English-medium share** |
|------------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Information & Communication Technologies | 78.4%                    |
| Engineering & Technology                 | 65.7%                    |
| Business, Economics & Law                | 55.4%                    |
| Natural Sciences & Mathematics           | 49.6%                    |
| Services, Tourism & Transport            | 47.8%                    |
| Arts & Humanities                        | 44.9%                    |
| Architecture & Urban Planning            | 36.8%                    |
| Education                                | 34.5%                    |
| Social Sciences & Journalism             | 33.1%                    |
| Healthcare & Medicine                    | 20.1%                    |
| Agriculture, Food & Veterinary           | 10.4%                    |

*Table 7.1 — English-medium share by subject area (raw-record basis, national).*

The nearly eight-fold spread — from 78.4% in ICT to 10.4% in Agriculture — means that “is this market English-medium” is not answerable as a single yes/no; the correct answer depends entirely on the subject area in question.

## 7.3 Variation by region

English-medium share also varies substantially by region, generally tracking the same regions with the deepest overall programme catalogues (see Section 9), with Almaty sitting close to the national average and several smaller, single-university regions sitting well below it — in some cases under 15%.

## 7.4 Variation by institution

At the institutional level, provision ranges from a small number of institutions offering their entire catalogue in English, to institutions offering almost none of theirs in English. Institutions with full or near-full English-medium catalogues are currently small by programme count, meaning full English-medium delivery is a proven but not yet scaled model in this market.

## 7.5 The English-medium price premium

Nationally, English-medium programmes carry a modest but statistically detectable price premium over non-English programmes (a positive correlation between English-medium status and tuition, significant at p \< 0.001). However, this premium is not consistent: when tuition is compared within the same degree level and subject area — the fairest like-for-like comparison available in this dataset — English-medium programmes price higher in roughly two-thirds of comparable combinations and lower in the remaining third. A blanket assumption that English-medium instruction commands a universal premium in this market would therefore be incorrect; the premium must be assessed field by field.

**So What?** English-medium capacity is not evenly spread across the system — it is concentrated in a handful of fields and institutions, which is exactly where international partnerships will cluster by default unless a partner deliberately looks elsewhere.

**Now What?** Institutions outside the existing English-medium hubs — particularly in Healthcare, Agriculture, and Education — may require targeted capacity building before they become viable international partners; this should be an explicit line item in any partnership feasibility assessment, not an assumed given.

# 8. Tuition Fee Analysis

## 8.1 Overall distribution

Domestic (RK-citizen) tuition fees across the dataset range from KZT 450,000 to KZT 14,125,800 per year, with a median of approximately KZT 1,200,000–1,278,000 depending on the deduplication basis used. The distribution is strongly right-skewed — a small number of very high-priced programmes (concentrated at Doctoral and Master's level at a small number of private institutions) pull the mean well above the median, meaning the median, not the mean, should be used as the representative benchmark for this market.

## 8.2 Fees by degree level

| **Degree level** | **Median annual tuition (KZT)** | **Mean annual tuition (KZT)** |
|------------------|---------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Bachelor’s       | 1,000,000                       | 1,271,059                     |
| Master’s         | 1,278,060                       | 1,402,631                     |
| Doctoral (PhD)   | 1,899,000                       | 1,941,793                     |
| Residency        | 1,811,300                       | 1,573,293                     |

*Table 8.1 — Domestic tuition by degree level (raw-record basis).*

Tuition rises consistently with degree level from Bachelor's to Doctoral, an intuitive and market-confirming pattern. Residency is an exception — its mean sits below its median, reflecting a narrow, tightly clustered price band rather than the wide spread seen at other levels.

## 8.3 Fees by subject area

Business, Economics & Law carries both the highest observed ceiling in the dataset (a single Doctoral programme at KZT 14,125,800) and one of the widest price ranges of any subject area, reflecting the coexistence of low-cost regional business programmes and a small number of premium private offerings within the same category. Architecture & Urban Planning has the lowest observed ceiling of any subject area (below KZT 1.9 million), consistent with its very small provider base (Section 6).

## 8.4 Data-quality caveats specific to pricing

- 13 institutions show zero price variance across their entire catalogue (see Section 5.4) — treat any single price point from these institutions as an institutional flat rate, not a differentiated market signal.

- International (foreign-student) fee data is incomplete: roughly one in five records lack a foreign-fee figure, and a small number of records (under 1% of comparable pairs) show a foreign fee lower than the domestic fee, which is logically inconsistent and likely reflects a data-entry error rather than an actual discount.

- Foreign fees are denominated in either KZT or USD within the same field, with the currency itself missing in roughly 12% of records — any cross-institution foreign-fee comparison must first confirm currency consistency (see Appendix D for the exchange-rate approach used where conversion was necessary).

## 8.5 Flat institutional pricing versus tiered pricing

Even among institutions with genuine per-programme price variation, pricing frequently clusters into a small number of repeating tiers rather than varying continuously by programme. At the largest technical university in the dataset, for example, Doctoral tuition clusters into three repeating price bands corresponding broadly to engineering/technical, applied-science, and humanities/social-science categories, rather than being individually set per programme. This tiered-by-category structure, rather than granular per-programme pricing, appears to be a common institutional pricing model in this market and should inform how any competitor price is interpreted.

# 9. Regional Analysis

This chapter examines how programme supply, degree-level availability, and pricing are distributed geographically — arguably the single most consequential dimension for a market-entry decision, since choice of city is typically a foreign institution's first strategic commitment.

## 9.1 Overall concentration

Almaty accounts for approximately 71.6% of all distinct programmes and 20 of the 46 universities in the dataset — by a wide margin the country's higher education centre. A concentration index (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index) computed across regions is roughly six times higher than the equivalent index computed across individual universities, confirming that geographic concentration, not institutional concentration, is the market's defining structural feature.

## 9.3 Degree-level availability by region

Master's and Doctoral programmes are heavily concentrated in Almaty; most other regions offer only a small fraction of the graduate-level catalogue available in the capital-adjacent centre. Medical Residency programmes appear in only two regions nationally (Almaty and Shymkent), meaning most regions cannot currently offer a complete academic pathway from Bachelor's through to clinical specialisation without requiring relocation.

## 9.4 Coverage gaps

Cross-tabulating the eleven subject areas against the sixteen mapped regions produces 176 possible combinations; roughly 45% of these combinations have zero recorded programmes. Several regions offer programmes in only one or two of the eleven subject areas. This fragmentation is the clearest quantitative evidence of unmet, geographically specific demand — though, as with all supply-side findings in this report, it indicates an absence of current provision, not confirmed unmet demand (see Section 14).

## 9.5 Price versus availability

Regional price variation is considerably smaller than regional availability variation. Regional median tuition varies roughly three-fold across regions with adequate sample size, while regional programme-count concentration is far more extreme (Section 9.1). In other words, once a region has any meaningful higher education supply at all, its prices are not radically different from the national pattern — the primary regional inequality in this market is one of catalogue breadth, not price.

**So What?** A regional entrant would not need to compete primarily on price — the more decisive factor is simply whether a credible catalogue exists in that region and subject area at all.

**Now What?** Regional expansion decisions should be driven first by the coverage-gap map in Section 9.4, and only secondarily by price positioning, since price is the smaller of the two regional variables in this market.

# 10. Market Segmentation

Combining the institutional-model finding of Section 5.3 with the pricing and English-medium patterns of Sections 7–8, four distinct institutional segments emerge from the data. These are descriptive clusters grounded in observed scale, specialisation, price tier, and English-medium share — not an official market classification — and are offered here because they are directly useful for positioning a new entrant.

### Segment A — Research-intensive generalists

Large state technical/research universities offering a broad catalogue across many subject areas, moderate-to-high English-medium share concentrated in technical fields, and mid-range pricing. This segment holds the largest raw share of the market and sets the de facto price benchmark for technical and engineering programmes.

### Segment B — Regional and pedagogical institutions

Smaller, single-region state or state-linked institutions with a heavy concentration in Education and applied technical fields, low English-medium share, and the lowest price tier in the market. This segment operates on a volume/access model rather than a differentiation model.

### Segment C — Private teaching and business universities

Metropolitan (predominantly Almaty) private institutions concentrated in Business, IT, and creative/media fields, with the widest range of price tiers in the market — from mid-range to the highest prices observed in the dataset — and generally higher, though not universally high, English-medium share.

### Segment D — Niche and international-facing specialists

A small number of institutions that are either single-subject specialists (100% of catalogue in one field, e.g. a dedicated arts or medical academy) or carry a fully or near-fully English-medium catalogue. This segment is currently small in scale but structurally distinct — it is the closest existing analogue in the market to how a new international entrant might itself be positioned.

| **Segment**                       | **Typical scale** | **Price tier** | **English-medium share** |
|-----------------------------------|-------------------|----------------|--------------------------|
| A — Research-intensive generalist | Very large        | Mid-range      | Moderate–high            |
| B — Regional / pedagogical        | Small–medium      | Lowest         | Low                      |
| C — Private teaching / business   | Medium–large      | Mid to highest | Mixed                    |

For a new international entrant, Segment D is the closest structural peer group in the current market, while Segments A and C represent the most direct existing competition for, respectively, technical/graduate and premium-private positioning.

# 11. Strategic Insights

Each insight below follows a consistent five-part structure — Observation, Evidence, Interpretation, Business Relevance, and Strategic Implication — so that verified fact, analytical judgement, and commercial relevance remain clearly distinguishable throughout. The Business Relevance step answers “so what?” for an international audience before the Strategic Implication answers “now what?”

### Insight 1 — Almaty is not one region among several; it is the market

**Observation.** Higher education supply in Kazakhstan is overwhelmingly concentrated in a single city.

**Evidence.** Almaty accounts for roughly 72% of distinct programmes and 20 of 46 universities; the regional concentration index is approximately six times higher than the equivalent institutional concentration index.

**Interpretation.** “Entering Kazakhstan” and “entering Almaty” are, for practical purposes, close to the same decision under current market conditions.

**Business Relevance — So What?** For an international university, this means brand visibility, competitor benchmarking, and partner due diligence should all be scoped at the city level, not the national level — a “Kazakhstan strategy” that is not explicitly an “Almaty strategy” risks being built on the wrong unit of analysis.

**Strategic Implication — Now What?** Any entry strategy premised on “national” Kazakhstan coverage should be explicitly reframed as an Almaty-first strategy, with regional expansion treated as a distinct, second-stage decision rather than a parallel one.

### Insight 2 — English-medium provision is a field-specific, not market-wide, phenomenon

**Observation.** English-medium instruction is heavily concentrated in a subset of technical and business fields.

**Evidence.** English-medium share ranges from 78.4% in Information & Communication Technologies to 10.4% in Agriculture, Food & Veterinary — a near eight-fold spread across the eleven subject areas.

**Interpretation.** A prospective entrant cannot assume general market readiness for English-medium delivery; readiness is concentrated in specific disciplines.

**Business Relevance — So What?** International partnerships will naturally gravitate toward the fields that already have English-language teaching capacity, meaning early partner conversations in low-English fields (Agriculture, Healthcare, Education) will likely encounter more friction and require more capacity-building than conversations in ICT or Engineering.

**Strategic Implication — Now What?** Subject-area selection should be treated as an internationalisation decision, not only an academic one — institutions choosing a low-English field should budget explicitly for English-medium capacity building rather than assuming it is already in place.

### Insight 3 — The English-medium price premium is real but not universal

**Observation.** English-medium programmes are priced higher than non-English programmes on average, but the relationship is inconsistent at a granular level.

**Evidence.** The national correlation between English-medium status and tuition is positive and statistically significant, yet when compared within the same degree level and subject area, English-medium programmes price higher in roughly two-thirds of comparable cases and lower in the remainder.

**Interpretation.** A single national English-medium pricing rule would misprice a meaningful share of the market.

**Business Relevance — So What?** A pricing team that assumes “English always commands a premium” and applies that assumption uniformly could unintentionally overprice an offering in the roughly one-third of fields where the relationship reverses, weakening competitiveness before the programme even launches.

**Strategic Implication — Now What?** Tuition strategy for an English-medium offering should be benchmarked against the specific subject-area and degree-level peer group, not against a market-wide average premium.

### Insight 4 — The market splits into two institutional models, with no stable middle

**Observation.** Institutions are either broad, moderately specialised generalists at large scale, or narrow, highly specialised institutions at small scale.

**Evidence.** Specialisation, measured across each institution's subject-area mix, clusters at the extremes: the largest institutions show low single-subject concentration, while several small institutions show 95–100% concentration in one field.

**Interpretation.** There is limited evidence in this market of a mid-sized, moderately diversified institutional model succeeding at scale.

**Business Relevance — So What?** An entrant that tries to be “a bit of everything” has no strong local precedent to draw confidence from, whereas a clearly specialist or clearly broad positioning both have visible, currently viable analogues in the market.

**Strategic Implication — Now What?** A new entrant should consciously choose between a narrow, defensible specialist position or a genuinely broad multi-field offering — a moderate, unfocused catalogue has the weakest precedent in the existing market structure.

### Insight 5 — Graduate-level provision outside Almaty is structurally thin

**Observation.** Master's and Doctoral programmes are heavily concentrated in the Almaty region, far more so than Bachelor's provision.

**Evidence.** The overwhelming majority of Master's and Doctoral programmes in the dataset are located in Almaty; most other regions retain only single-digit or low double-digit counts of graduate-level programmes.

**Interpretation.** Regional demand for graduate education, if it exists, is currently very poorly served by local supply.

**Business Relevance — So What?** This is the single clearest, most quantifiable regional white space identified in this report — an international partner seeking differentiated, lower-competition positioning does not need to look at a crowded Almaty Bachelor's market to find one.

**Strategic Implication — Now What?** A graduate-level (Master's or Doctoral) offering located outside Almaty would face materially less direct local competition than an equivalent Bachelor's-level offering in the same city.

### Insight 6 — Competitive intensity varies sharply, and predictably, by subject area

**Observation.** Some subject areas are served by more than five times as many distinct providers as others.

**Evidence.** Business, Economics & Law (27 providers) and Engineering & Technology (26 providers) are the most contested fields nationally; Architecture & Urban Planning (5 providers), Healthcare & Medicine (12), and Agriculture, Food & Veterinary (15) are the least contested.

**Interpretation.** The most “obvious” fields for a new entrant to consider — business and engineering — are also the most competitively saturated.

**Business Relevance — So What?** Instinctive field choices (business, engineering) will put a new entrant in the most crowded part of the market on day one; the thin-provider fields are less obvious precisely because they have attracted less attention, which is itself a competitive opening rather than a warning sign.

**Strategic Implication — Now What?** Thin-provider fields merit serious evaluation alongside the more obvious high-demand fields, provided real-world demand (outside the scope of this dataset) can be independently validated.

### Insight 7 — A meaningful share of observed pricing may not reflect genuine per-programme differentiation

**Observation.** A notable subset of institutions charge an identical tuition fee across every programme they offer.

**Evidence.** 13 of 43 priced institutions show zero price variance across their full catalogue; at the largest technical university, even genuinely varying Doctoral prices cluster into just three repeating tiers rather than continuous per-programme pricing.

**Interpretation.** Competitor pricing benchmarks drawn from this market should be read at the tier or institutional level, not assumed to reflect fine-grained, programme-specific cost structures.

**Business Relevance — So What?** A pricing team benchmarking against a flat-fee competitor risks anchoring a specific programme's price to a number that was never actually set for that programme, producing a misleading competitive comparison.

**Strategic Implication — Now What?** Any pricing strategy built from this dataset's competitor prices should explicitly identify which comparator institutions use flat or tiered pricing before those prices are used as a direct benchmark.

# 12. Opportunities & Risks

## 12.1 Opportunities

### Development of English-medium provision in underserved fields

Fields with both low current English-medium share and a plausible link to international demand — for example, Healthcare & Medicine (20.1% English-medium) and Agriculture, Food & Veterinary (10.4%) — represent the clearest whitespace for an English-medium offering, since incumbents in these fields have not yet built English-medium delivery capacity at scale.

### International partnerships anchored in existing English-medium capacity

A small number of institutions already operate fully or near-fully English-medium catalogues. These institutions represent lower-friction partnership entry points than building English-medium delivery capacity from zero within a predominantly non-English institution.

### Regional expansion into graduate-level education

The graduate-level supply gap outside Almaty (Insight 5) is the most quantifiable regional opportunity identified in this analysis, particularly in regions that already host at least one functioning university (reducing basic infrastructure risk) but currently offer little or no Master's/Doctoral provision.

### Differentiated positioning in thin-provider subject areas

Architecture & Urban Planning, Healthcare & Medicine, and Agriculture & Veterinary Sciences combine low national provider counts with multi-regional presence at low volume per region — a pattern of dispersed, thin supply that a focused, well-resourced entrant could credibly consolidate around.

## 12.2 Risks

### Extreme geographic concentration limits addressable market outside Almaty

Any strategy not centred on Almaty will, by construction, be operating in a market with materially less existing infrastructure, competitor benchmark data, and — on the evidence available — likely less immediate demand density.

### Data-quality risk in competitor benchmarking

As documented throughout Sections 3, 5, and 8, a meaningful share of the source registry (duplicate records, flat institutional pricing, incomplete foreign-fee data, non-unique classification codes) requires careful handling. A market-entry plan built on face-value competitor figures from this registry risks anchoring on artefacts rather than genuine market prices.

### Uneven and inconsistent English-medium pricing dynamics

Because the English-medium premium reverses in a meaningful minority of subject-area/level combinations (Insight 3), a pricing strategy assuming a uniform international premium could misprice a new offering in either direction depending on the chosen field.

### Supply gaps are not verified demand

Every regional and subject-area “gap” identified in this report reflects an absence of current supply, observed from a registry of existing programmes. This dataset contains no enrolment, application, or demand-side information, and gaps should not be treated as confirmed opportunities until validated against independent demand evidence (see Section 14).

# 14. Limitations

This report is built on a single, consolidated snapshot of a national programme registry, and every finding in it should be read with the following limitations in mind.

### 14.1 Snapshot in time

The dataset represents a point-in-time extraction of the registry and does not capture how programmes, prices, or English-medium status may have changed since. Programme registration dates within the source data span 2019 to 2026 and do not behave as a reliable indicator of when a price or programme was last genuinely updated (Section 3.3).

### 14.2 Inconsistent institutional update practices

Institutions appear to update their registry entries at very different frequencies and levels of completeness. This means apparent differences in “recency” or “activity” between institutions may reflect differences in administrative practice rather than differences in genuine programme development.

### 14.3 Registry-to-website synchronisation is not verified

This report does not independently verify that every registry entry matches current information on the corresponding university's own website. Discrepancies are plausible and, where a specific figure is decision-critical, should be confirmed directly with the institution.

### 14.4 Region is an inferred, not native, field

Every regional finding in this report (Section 9, and any insight or recommendation referencing a specific region) rests on an external mapping of university name to home city, not a field present in the source registry. The full mapping is provided in Appendix A for independent verification.

### 14.5 Non-unique classification codes

As documented in Section 6.3, OPVO classification codes are not guaranteed unique across institutions. Any external cross-referencing of this report's findings against the source registry must confirm programme identity by institution and title, not code alone.

### 14.6 Supply-side data only

This dataset describes what is offered and at what listed price — it contains no information on enrolment, applications, acceptance rates, graduate outcomes, or actual demand. Every “gap” or “opportunity” identified in this report is a statement about current supply, not a validated statement about unmet demand, and should be treated accordingly by any reader making an investment or entry decision.

### 14.7 Data-quality indicators requiring independent verification

- 13 institutions with zero price variance across their full catalogue (possible flat fee or incomplete field — Section 5.4).

- Approximately 20% missing foreign-student fee data, with mixed currency denomination (Section 8.4).

- A verified, non-trivial error rate found when a small independent sample of specific registry cross-references was checked during the preparation of this report — readers relying on any single specific figure for a material decision should verify it directly against the primary registry before use.

# 15. Conclusion

## 15.1 Current state of the market

Kazakhstan's higher education market is large by regional standards, structurally concentrated in a single city, and internally more differentiated — by price, English-medium provision, and institutional model — than a first glance at national averages would suggest. It is a market with genuine, identifiable structure rather than a uniform, homogenous system, and that structure is now visible in a way it was not before this consolidated analysis existed.

## 15.2 Outlook

The most credible near-term opportunities identified in this report — graduate-level expansion outside Almaty, English-medium development in currently underserved fields, and differentiated positioning in thin-provider subject areas — are all evidence-backed but supply-side observations. Their conversion into genuine market opportunities depends on demand-side validation that lies outside the scope of this dataset.

## 15.3 This report within the wider research programme

This assessment is designed as the first analytical layer — Work Package 1 (WP1): Higher Education Landscape — of a broader Kazakhstan Higher Education Market Intelligence programme. By establishing a structured, evidence-based understanding of the national programme landscape, it provides the foundation for three connected work packages:

- WP2 — International Student Mobility: cross-referencing this supply-side registry against UNESCO UIS and UN DESA international student mobility statistics, to test whether Kazakhstan's inbound/outbound student flows align with the supply gaps identified here.

- WP3 — Demographic Outlook: incorporating demographic projections for Kazakhstan's university-age population, to assess whether regional supply gaps identified in WP1 are likely to close organically or require external investment.

- WP4 — Labour Market Alignment: layering in labour-market and graduate-outcome data, to test whether the subject areas with the thinnest current supply (Section 9.4, Insight 6) also show labour-market signals consistent with unmet demand, rather than simply low provider interest.

Each work package answers a distinct question that this report, by design, cannot: WP1 establishes what the market currently offers; WP2–WP4 establish who is moving through it, how the applicant pool will change, and what the labour market actually rewards. Together, these complementary research streams are intended to build a single, comprehensive evidence base — the final Kazakhstan Higher Education Market Intelligence report — to support strategic decision-making by universities, policymakers, investors, and international partners.

# Appendix C — Sources and Cleaning Methodology

### Sources

- OPVO/EPVO national programme classification registry (primary source for all programme, pricing, and English-medium data).

- Publicly available information on university campus locations, used solely to construct the region mapping in Appendix A.

### Cleaning methodology summary

- Column realignment: the raw export required correction of a column-order mismatch between its labelled headers and its actual field order.

- Character-encoding normalisation: identification of Cyrillic-character substitutions within Latin-alphabet classification codes, affecting approximately 3% of codes.

- Deduplication: rows were grouped by (university, OPVO code, degree level) to identify and quantify duplicate registry entries; all headline market-size figures in this report use the resulting deduplicated count.

- Date validation: registration dates were parsed and validated against a standard date format; no records fell outside a plausible range.

# Appendix D — Currency Note

All tuition figures in this report are presented in Kazakhstani tenge (KZT) as recorded in the source registry, unless otherwise stated. Where foreign-student fees were recorded in US dollars, no fixed exchange rate has been applied within this report's KZT-denominated tables; any USD-denominated figures are labelled explicitly where they appear. Readers converting figures to another currency should apply a current exchange rate at the time of use, since tuition figures in this dataset span multiple pricing years (2024–2025 through 2026–2027) and are not adjusted for exchange-rate movements over that period.
