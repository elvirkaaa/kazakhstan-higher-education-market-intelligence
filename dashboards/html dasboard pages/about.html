PurposeWhy this project exists

AZM Education is a consultancy focused on Kazakhstan and Central Asia's higher-education sector. This project was built to answer a specific market-intelligence question for the consultancy: where is Kazakhstan's higher-education supply concentrated, how internationalised is it, what demographic pressures exist, and where do supply and demand visibly diverge? It combines a public programme registry, government mobility and demographic statistics, and international data sources into one connected analytical product, rather than treating each dataset in isolation.

Data & MethodsWhat this draws on

Four independently-sourced datasets — the OPVO/EPVO programme registry, BNS demographic and migration tables, MSHE RK/ENIC-Kazakhstan mobility reports, and UNESCO/UN DESA cross-checks — cleaned, deduplicated, and joined via a region crosswalk built to reconcile Kazakhstan's 2018 and 2022 administrative boundary reforms. Full pipeline, validation checks, and every documented data-quality issue are on Data & Methods and Methodology.

Key FindingsWhat the analysis found

  • Higher-education programme supply is heavily concentrated: 70.7% of the 2,417 distinct programmes identified are in Almaty (regional HHI 5,176; institutional HHI not calculated — full 46-university data unavailable)
  • Internationalisation runs through 9+ structurally distinct channels (full-degree, exchange, Bolashak, institutional partnerships) with different dominant partner countries — no single "internationalisation" figure represents all of them
  • Demographic growth is geographically uneven (natural growth ranges from +17.4 to -2.7 per 1,000 across regions) and does not track programme supply: youth share and supply share correlate weakly and, in one robustness check (Spearman vs. Pearson), the apparent link with migration turned out to be driven by two outlier regions
  • Six regions combine above-median youth population share with below-median recorded programme supply — a spatial pattern worth further investigation, not evidence of unmet demand

Full evidence, caveats, and the distinction between what's observed vs. interpreted are on Strategic Implications.

LimitationsWhat this project cannot show

Programme counts are not enrolment or capacity; the youth (0-15) indicator is demographic context, not a direct forecast of university-age demand; migration data captures all movement, not student-specific movement; and the Supply × Demand comparison identifies spatial patterns, not causation or unmet demand. None of these are hidden — each page states its own limitations next to its findings.
