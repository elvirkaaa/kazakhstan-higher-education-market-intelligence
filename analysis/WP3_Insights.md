# WP3 Insights

Insight-mapping stage: each row is a candidate finding for the WP3 report, traced back to `analysis/WP3_Analytical_Notes.md` and the underlying processed data, with a confidence rating.

**Confidence key:** *High* = confirmed by 2+ independent sources and/or internally reconciling multi-year data. *Medium* = a single well-evidenced source, or a pattern visible in only one/few years. *Low* = suggestive but thin or unresolved evidence.

| # | Insight | Evidence | Source(s) | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Kazakhstan's population has grown consistently since the mid-1990s, following a post-independence decline | Population fell from the early 1990s before recovering; World Bank total population 1960-2025 shows a clear trough in the 1990s followed by sustained growth to 20.8M+ by 2025 | World Bank | High |
| 2 | Urbanisation has risen steadily and without reversal for over 60 years | 44.7% (1960) to 62.4% (2025), monotonic across the entire series with no reversals - one of the cleanest trends in the whole WP3 dataset | World Bank | High |
| 3 | Fertility has fallen in a classic demographic-transition pattern, closely mirrored by the dependency ratio's long decline | TFR fell from 4.5 (1960) to under 3.0 (2024); total dependency ratio fell from 59.9 (1990) through the 1990s-2010s before beginning to rise again toward 2026 (61.8) | World Bank + UN WPP | High |
| 4 | The youth/applicant-age cohort (15-24) declined for over three decades but may now be reversing | 2.93M (1990) declining to a low of ~2.82M (2024), with UN WPP projecting an increase to ~3.08M by 2026 | UN WPP | Medium |
| 5 | Kazakhstan's own official youth definition (14-28) differs meaningfully from the international standard (15-24) used elsewhere in this project | BNS/TALDAU's national age-structure data explicitly reports a "14-28 years" composite band (5,886,755 in 2025) as a distinct, officially-tracked category | BNS/TALDAU | High |
| 6 | Independent national-level age-band figures from two different agencies agree closely, increasing confidence in both | BNS/TALDAU 2025: 15-19 = 1,705,096, 20-24 = 1,260,061; UN WPP 2025: 15-19 = 1,682,344, 20-24 = 1,262,465 - within 1.3% and 0.2% respectively | BNS/TALDAU + UN WPP | High |
| 7 | Internal (interregional) migration shows a clear pattern of concentration toward the three republican-significance cities | Astana (+87,459), Almaty (+33,126), and Shymkent (+15,130) show the largest net internal in-migration; Turkistan region (-42,649) and Zhambyl (-21,179) show the largest net internal out-migration | BNS (single latest year, ~2024/2025) | Medium |
| 8 | The three regions created in the 2022 territorial reform are too new to assess long-run demographic trends independently | Abai, Zhetisu, and Ulytau show blank/dash data before 2022 across every BNS regional time series (births, deaths, natural growth, migration) - only 2-4 years of independent data exist for any of them | BNS | High (as a data-limitation finding) |

## Cross-Work-Package Insights (WP1 × WP3)

These insights connect WP3's demographic findings directly to WP1's higher-education supply data (8,530 raw OPVO records, ~2,417 distinct programmes, 46 universities). They are necessarily more inferential than single-work-package insights, since they combine two independently collected datasets — confidence ratings reflect this.

| # | Insight | Evidence | Source(s) | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | **Higher-education supply is geographically inverted relative to where the youth population is concentrated.** Turkistan region has the highest child population share (0-15) of any region in Kazakhstan (39.5%, vs. a 31.2% national average) — yet WP1 found it holds only 1.0% of the country's distinct higher-education programmes. | WP1: Turkistan = 1.0% of national programme supply (report Table 9.1). WP3: Turkistan = 39.5% aged 0-15, the highest of all 20 administrative units, computed directly from BNS regional age structure (beginning of 2024) | WP1 + WP3 (BNS) | High — both figures are precise, directly computed, and the gap is large enough to be robust to reasonable measurement error |
| 13 | **The region with the second-largest higher-education supply has a below-average, ageing population profile — supply is not chasing the youngest regions.** Ust-Kamenogorsk / East Kazakhstan holds 10.0% of national programme supply (WP1's second-largest regional share, after Almaty) but has the second-lowest youth (0-15) share of any region (22.8%, vs. 31.2% national average). | WP1: Ust-Kamenogorsk = 10.0% of programme supply. WP3: East Kazakhstan (Shygys Kazakhstan) = 22.8% aged 0-15, second-lowest nationally | WP1 + WP3 (BNS) | High |
| 14 | **Almaty's dominant supply position is not explained by it having the country's youngest population — the opposite is true.** Almaty city's youth (0-15) share (27.4%) sits below the national average (31.2%), yet Almaty holds 71.6% of national higher-education programme supply. Supply concentration in Almaty appears to be driven by factors other than local demographic demand (e.g. institutional legacy, agglomeration effects, the internal-migration pull documented in Insight #7) rather than by Almaty simply having more young people to educate locally. | WP1: Almaty = 71.6% of programme supply. WP3: Almaty city = 27.4% aged 0-15, below the 31.2% national average; Almaty is also a top internal-migration destination (+33,126 net, Insight #7) | WP1 + WP3 (BNS) | High |
| 15 | **The 30-year national decline in the university-applicant-age cohort likely reinforced, rather than caused, geographic concentration.** A shrinking national 15-24 cohort (Insight #4) makes small, dispersed regional expansions economically harder to sustain than consolidating supply in one large, migration-attracting hub — consistent with (though not proof of) why supply concentrated in Almaty rather than following population distribution. This is a plausible mechanism linking WP3's demographic trend to WP1's structural finding, not a directly tested causal claim. | WP3: 15-24 cohort declined from 2.93M (1990) to ~2.82M (2024). WP1: Almaty = 71.6% of supply; HHI-based concentration analysis (WP1 report) found regional concentration (HHI 6,008) far exceeds institutional concentration (HHI 1,000) | WP1 + WP3 (interpretive, not directly evidenced) | Low — plausible mechanism, not directly tested |
| 16 | **If the projected 2024-2026 youth-cohort reversal (Insight #4) continues, it would arrive precisely in the regions least prepared to serve it.** The regions with the youngest current populations and highest natural-growth potential (Turkistan, Mangystau, Kyzylorda, Atyrau, Shymkent city — all 35%+ youth share) are simultaneously among the regions with the thinnest higher-education supply in WP1's dataset. A rebounding national youth cohort would disproportionately materialise in exactly these under-supplied regions. | Combines WP3 regional youth-share ranking (Turkistan 39.5%, Mangystau 38.7%, Shymkent city 37.4%, Kyzylorda 36.4%, Atyrau 35.8%) with WP1's regional supply data (all five regions individually accounted for only a small fraction of national programme supply, well below their population share) | WP1 + WP3 (BNS) | Medium — the youth-share figures are precise; the forward-looking "would disproportionately materialise" claim is an extrapolation |

### Why this matters for the integrated report

Insights #12-14 are, individually, three of the most concrete, quantified findings produced anywhere in this project so far — each pairs one exact WP1 percentage with one exact WP3 percentage and shows a clear, large, directionally consistent mismatch. Together they support a single strategic narrative: **Kazakhstan's higher-education supply is not distributed according to where its youth population actually is or is growing** — worth a dedicated subsection in the final integrated Kazakhstan Higher Education Market Intelligence report (WP1+WP2+WP3), not just a passing mention in WP3 alone.

## Notes on using this table

- Insights #8 and #9 are meta-findings about the evidence base itself (data-limitation findings), not substantive claims about Kazakhstan's demography - both belong in the report's Methodology/Limitations sections, consistent with how equivalent findings were handled in WP2 (see WP2 Insight #11 for the precedent).
- Insight #4 (the projected youth-cohort reversal) is the single most report-relevant finding for RQ3 and the "Higher Education Implications" section specifically - if it holds up in the longer 2030-2050 projection horizon (not yet checked against the Worldometer table's later years), it would directly support a narrative of stabilising or growing future domestic student demand.
- Insight #10 is the closest available substitute for a true regional youth breakdown (per the documented gap in Insight #9) - it should be presented explicitly as a coarser proxy, not as equivalent to a genuine 17-24 regional series.
- No insight in this table yet draws on a BNS-vs-World-Bank population cross-check (identified as a planned validation step in Research Design.md but not yet completed) - this remains open before the report is finalised.
